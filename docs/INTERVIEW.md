# Interview notes: ad-creative-agent

Personal project, fictional game, synthetic data. Numbers come from `results/` and `runs/`. Prices are assumed list prices in `adcreative/llm.py`, so costs are estimates.

## 1. What is it, in 30 seconds?
It takes a launch brief and returns nine ad concepts: three audiences with three hooks each. Each concept has copy for Meta, TikTok and Google UAC, an image, and a critic score. A ranker picks the top six and gives a reason for each. Code enforces platform length limits and bans risky claims. The product is a fictional puzzle game, Lantern Drift. One full run took 165.5 seconds and an estimated $0.36 (`runs/lantern-drift-20261024-020522/manifest.json`).

## 2. Why a state machine and not an agent loop?
The order of steps never changes, so a loop that decides what to do next adds cost and failure modes for nothing. Each step is one function over a shared state dict, and the manifest keeps a trace of seconds and dollars per step (`adcreative/pipeline.py`). Where the work is judgment (angles, copy, critique) a model does it. Where the work is a rule (limits, gates, ranking) code does it. I would use a loop only if a step needed to decide to retry with a different tool, which none do here.

## 3. Why this model routing?
The judge is unvalidated against humans, and the platform-fit score is unreliable.

Haiku parses the brief and repairs over-long copy, because both are small and checkable. Sonnet writes angles, copy and art direction, and reads the images. Opus is used only as the judge of copy in evals and in the two optional check passes (`critic_check`, `judge_check`). A judge from a stronger tier than the writer lowers the chance that it likes its own style. The vision critic is different: it runs on Sonnet, the same model family as the writer, so they can share its blind spots. I chose Sonnet there for cost and because it reads images. The seeded vision test (section 8) is the only check on it, and it is 16 images that I made myself. Copy writing was the biggest cost ($2.29 of $5.97 total, from `results/cost_log.jsonl`) and the judge was second ($2.04), so routing the judge to Opus was a real cost choice, not a free one. The copy judge is already Opus, so the writer and the judge are different tiers. A second Opus pass over the judge was planned and not run (section 4). A check on the Opus judge, not independent: a Claude rater (not a human) scored 20 items blind, Spearman 0.733 on hook strength and -0.141 on platform fit (`results/hand_score.json`). A second Opus pass was not run, because of the cap.

## 4. How was the LLM judge calibrated?
The judge is unvalidated against humans. Do not read "calibrated" as validated.
I wrote ten copy sets for Lantern Drift by hand: five good and five with one planted flaw each (w1 plants two weak dimensions: a vague hook and weak clarity, two policy breaches, a platform-fit miss, a clarity miss). The judge scored all ten in one call per pass, two passes, with a different order. It found the planted flaw in 5 of 5 weak sets and scored all dimensions at 6 or more in 4 of 5 strong sets. Mean score was 8.02 on strong and 3.27 on weak, and scores moved 0.35 points on average between passes (`results/judge_calibration.json`). The one strong set that missed gave a hook score of 5.0. I read that as a strict judge, not a broken one. The limit is that ten items on one product is a smoke test, not validation. It is also circular: I planted the flaws and I wrote the judge prompt, so the judge may find flaws that I know how to describe. Independent hand scores from someone else would be stronger evidence, and I did not collect them. Update: I wrote a second-model check (`python -m adcreative judge-check`): a blind re-score of 20 items, stratified by the first judge score, with Spearman against the first judge. I did not run it. The project had about $0.09 left under its $6 cap and the run costs more, so there is no correlation number. When it runs, read it with care: the first judge is already claude-opus-5-5, so a second Opus pass measures repeatability, not independence, and model-vs-model agreement is not human agreement. The 20-item sheet (`data/hand_score_sheet.csv`) is now filled, but by Claude at the owner's request, blind to the judge's scores, not by a human. Spearman against the judge (`results/hand_score.json`, n=20): hook strength 0.733, platform fit -0.141. So the judge and the Claude rater rank hook strength alike, and do not rank platform fit alike; Claude's fit mean is 6.6 against the judge's 5.25. Both are Claude models and n is 20, so this is a rough check, not human validation. The Opus re-score of the same 20 did not run: about $0.09 of the cap was left. Opus agreement: no number.

## 5. What does the judge score, and what does it not?
Four dimensions on 0 to 10: hook strength, clarity, policy safety and platform fit. It sees only the copy, the facts and the "not true" list. It never sees the deterministic check results, so the two signals stay independent. It does not measure real ad performance. Nothing in this repo shows that a high score predicts clicks or installs, and I would not claim it.

## 6. What failed or looked weak?
Five things. First, an early copy call returned empty text. These models think by default and thinking tokens use the `max_tokens` budget (see `THINK_HEADROOM` in `adcreative/llm.py`; the `debug` scope in `results/cost_log.jsonl` cost $0.0746). I added headroom, and the ledger now records the stop reason. Second, judge platform fit is low (mean 5.2 over 12 briefs, below 5.5 in 9 of 12, `results/copy_eval.json`). I cannot tell weak copy from a strict judge. A Claude rater, not a human, scored the 20-item blind sheet: it agrees with the judge on hook strength (Spearman 0.733) and not on platform fit (-0.141, `results/hand_score.json`). That points at the platform-fit score as the shaky one, but it is no human proof. So the 6.66 headline is a relative score, not proof the copy is good. Third, the fallback images are flat vector, and the critic says so. Fourth, the pipeline's own images are placeholders and no image API has run; the 6 ChatGPT images were made by hand, and the demo says so on the first screen. Fifth, the vision test is 16 images I made myself (15 defects, 1 control): 15 of 15 blocked, control passed. It is an easy test, and code catches the aspect-ratio cases.

## 7. How do you stop the model from breaking platform limits?
The prompt states the limits. Then code checks every headline, text and CTA (`check_limits`). If anything fails, one Haiku call shortens it, and a deterministic trim at a word boundary is the last step. In the live copy eval 0 of 108 concepts had a raw violation, so I forced it once with tiny limits on real Lantern Drift copy: 6 raw violations, the Haiku repair fixed all, 0 left (`results/repair_live.json`, $0.0069). Offline tests with a mocked LLM also cover it (`tests/test_agents.py`).

## 8. How does the vision critic catch text typos and misleading claims?
The critic copies out every word it sees in the image. Code compares those words with the words we meant to draw, ignoring case, order and punctuation (`overlay_matches`). A separate score covers policy safety, with the brief's "not true" list in the prompt. Two more gates came with the bigger test: legibility below 5 blocks a concept, and code blocks any image that is not 1:1. I tested the critic on 16 images with known defects: 4 typos, 5 misleading or banned claims, 3 low-contrast, 3 wrong aspect ratio, and 1 clean control. It blocked all 15 defects and passed the control (`results/vision_seeded.json`). The aspect-ratio ones are caught by code, so only 12 of the 15 test the model. I made the defects, so this is an easy test, and one control says nothing about false alarms. An Opus run on the same images was skipped to stay under the cost cap, so I have no model-vs-model agreement number.

## 9. How does the creative memory work, and does it help?
24 invented past concept notes are embedded locally with bge-small. The six nearest notes go into the angle agent's prompt as angles to avoid. Then every new angle is embedded and compared with all notes. Above cosine 0.78, the agent redoes those angles once. Across three trials per arm, angles over the threshold were 9 with memory off and 0 with memory on, and mean similarity was 0.751 with memory off vs 0.709 with memory on (`results/memory_demo.json`). I chose 0.78 after seeing the memory-off numbers, so it is tuned on the same brief. Three trials is small.

## 10. Why did the image step use SVG and not diffusion? It is free locally.
The pipeline's image API has not run. No working OpenAI key was available (the only one returned 401), and no account was created. The SVG fallback is the only image path the pipeline ran. Later, on 2026-10-24, I made 6 real images by hand: I pasted the top 6 Tidebreak art-director prompts into ChatGPT (GPT image) in the web app, one new chat each, and downloaded the results. The vision critic scored them (means: legibility 6.0, brand fit 7.7, policy safety 8.3, message match 7.2; `results/vision_real_gpt.json`). That is 6 images, one pass, no text drawn, so it shows the critic works on real output and says nothing about GPT image quality in general.

It is free only if a GPU server is available. No local Stable Diffusion server answered the probe on ports 7860 and 8188 at run time. I had no working image key. So the live runs used the SVG fallback: typography plus procedural SVG rendered in headless Chrome. The backend choice is one function (`detect_backend`). An OpenAI adapter and an A1111-style diffusion adapter exist; the A1111 and ComfyUI adapters are tested against hand-built response fixtures (`tests/test_pipeline.py`) and neither ran live. For video I added a small motion step (`adcreative/motion.py`): a 6.5 s ffmpeg zoom and crossfade over the top 3 images, kept at `docs/assets/motion.mp4`. It is not video generation. The vision critic has only seen flat vector art, so I make no claim about real imagery. The next step is one 9-image pass on a GPU box or with a personal image key, then the critic on those images.

## 11. What would you change for production?
Call a real image model through the adapter inside the pipeline (the 6 GPT images were made by hand), and re-run the critic on more than 6 real images. Learn memory from performance data instead of hand-written notes. Replace house length limits with the current limits per placement. Run the eval more than once per brief to get a spread. Add human review before anything is published. Cache the brief parse and the memory index. And measure whether judge scores track real results, which is the main open question.

## 12. What does it cost?
Estimated, from `results/cost_log.jsonl` and `results/copy_eval.json`. A full run with images: $0.3584 for Lantern Drift (18 calls) and $0.3376 for Tidebreak (17 calls). A copy-only brief with the judge: mean $0.3472, about $0.199 for the pipeline and $0.148 for the judge. The judge calibration cost $0.2634 and the 16-image seeded vision test $0.112 (the earlier 4-image test cost $0.0368). The whole project, including failed attempts, came to $5.97 against a $6 cap. Real billing may differ, since the prices in the repo are assumed list prices, not checked against billing.
